Personal Interest

“I keep floss at my desk, because I forget so often at night. Hearing friends and family talk about skipping it, and catching myself doing the same, made me realize this is common, and overlooked.”

I like designing from real behavior and small daily frustrations, turning them into low-pressure, realistic habits that survive the version of you awake at 11pm.

How Might We

Help people who struggle to floss build a simple daily habit, so they can improve their oral hygiene and feel more confident about their smile?

Background Research

This isn't a small habit to skip.

If you skip it
  • Gum disease starts with swollen, red, bleeding gums. Left alone, it can spread to the bone and loosen teeth.1
  • It is associated with heart disease and diabetes,1 and with dementia in older adults.2 These are links found in studies, not proof that it causes them.
  • Toothbrush bristles can't clean the tight spaces between teeth.3
  • Only about 1 in 3 US adults floss every day.4
What actually works
  • Tying flossing to a set cue builds the habit. People who flossed right after brushing had stronger habits 8 months later.5
  • Tracking progress helps people reach their goals.6
  • Sharing that progress with others makes tracking work even better.6
  • Habits run on a loop: a cue, a routine, then a reward.7
  • Starting tiny and attaching it to something you already do helps it stick.8

Numbers match the Sources page at the end.

Competitors

Dental apps remind you. Habit games reward you. None of them does both for flossing.

I compared five apps feature by feature, using each app's own listing. Reminders and timers are solved. Rewards and friends exist, but only in a general habit game or behind a smart toothbrush. Flosser puts both into a free, two-minute flossing routine.

03 · The System

What happens when you stop?

Based on consecutive missed days. The grime happens to Chip, and he wears it patiently.

Three signals, three speeds. The streak holds where you left it, so nothing already earned is taken back. Snaggle moves in weeks, slow enough that a bad patch is never a verdict. Friends see your progress, and that is the part that actually keeps people honest.

Branding Guidelines

The mascots are the brand.

Flosser's identity isn't a logo and a palette. It's two characters and one rule about who gets blamed.

Chip

Reacts. Never blames.

He dulls when the habit slips and brightens when it returns. He never reports a number and never asks where you've been.

Snaggle

Takes the blame. Always.

Skipping a day is what lets him in. From there, the grime he brings lands on Chip, not on a stat or a scolding message, so the consequence stays visual instead of preachy.

The rule underneath

Grime lands on Chip, never in a sentence pointed at the user. Leaving him unguarded is what let Snaggle in.

Reflection

It started as a reminder. It became a habit loop.

What changed

The original idea was a flossing reminder. The final product became a habit system, and every screen exists to move someone through that loop without friction or guilt.

Remember→Act→Reward→Return
What I learned
  1. 01

    Test the interaction, not just the screen

    A clear interface can still fail when the interaction doesn't match what someone expects.

  2. 02

    Motivation and punishment aren't the same thing

    Chip and Snaggle taught me a game can create urgency without making anyone feel guilty.

  3. 03

    Small friction matters in habit products

    When the habit takes under two minutes, one extra decision is a reason to skip it.

What's next

Test Chip and Snaggle with a bigger group to see if the mascot holds up after the first week, and check whether Friends actually keeps people flossing, not just whether they can find it.
